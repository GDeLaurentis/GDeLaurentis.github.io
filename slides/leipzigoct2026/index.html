$\circ$ There are two sources of redundancies: kinematic constraints & Schouten/Gram identities.

Factoriality of Massless Spinor Rings
$\circ$ Throughout we have referred to the irreducible denominator factors $\mathcal{D}_i$.
$\phantom{\circ}$ But is the factorization $\mathcal{D}=\prod_i \mathcal{D}_i$ actually unique? i.e. is $R_n$ factorial?
$\circ$ $R_3$ is not even a domain: it has zero divisors,
$$ s_{12}=\langle12\rangle[21]=s_3=0, \qquad \langle12\rangle\neq0,\quad [21]\neq0 . $$
$\circ$ $R_4$ is a domain: the momentum-cons. ideal $J_4=\left\langle\sum_i |i\rangle[i|\right\rangle$ is prime, but $R_4$ is not factorial,
$$ \langle12\rangle[12]=\langle34\rangle[34]. $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ Shows two inequivalent factorizations into non-associate irreducibles.
$\phantom{\circ}$ $\Rightarrow$ 4 point reduced denominators need not be unique: e.g. Parke-Taylor MHV = anti-MHV.
Theorem: $R_n$ is a unique factorization domain for every $n\geq5$.
Integrality for $n\geq4$
$\circ$ Criterion: if a complete-intersection variety contains an integral open subset whose complement has smaller dimension, then the whole coordinate ring is a domain.
$$ R_n= \mathbb F[\Lambda,\widetilde\Lambda]/ \langle\Lambda\widetilde\Lambda\rangle , \qquad \Lambda= \begin{pmatrix} a_1&\cdots&a_n\\ b_1&\cdots&b_n \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \widetilde\Lambda= \begin{pmatrix} c_1&d_1\\ \vdots&\vdots\\ c_n&d_n \end{pmatrix}. $$
$\circ$ Stratify by $r=\operatorname{rank}\Lambda$. A $2\times n$ matrix of rank $r$ depends on $r(n+2-r)$ parameters, while $\dim\ker\Lambda=n-r$, thus
$$ \begin{array}{c|c} r & \dim\{(\Lambda,\widetilde\Lambda):\Lambda\widetilde\Lambda=0\}\\ \hline 2 & 2n+2(n-2)=4n-4\\ 1 & (n+1)+2(n-1)=3n-1\\ 0 & 2n \end{array} $$
$\circ$ Why is the rank-two locus integral? On a chart where $\Delta=\langle12\rangle\neq0$, we can solve
$$ \underbrace{ \begin{pmatrix} a_1&a_2\\ b_1&b_2 \end{pmatrix} }_{M\ {\rm invertible}} \begin{pmatrix} c_1\\ c_2 \end{pmatrix} = -\sum_{i=3}^n c_i \begin{pmatrix} a_i\\ b_i \end{pmatrix}. $$
$3n-1 < 4n-4 \iff n\geq4 \;\; \Longrightarrow \; R_{n\geq 4}$ is a domain.
Choosing a Prime Divisor
$\circ$ For the next step we need a prime element, $\Delta$. Say,
$$ \Delta=\langle12\rangle=a_1b_2-a_2b_1. $$
$\circ$ We study the quotient $R_n/(\Delta)$, i.e. the momentum-conservation variety restricted to $\langle12\rangle=0$.
$\circ$ On the full-rank locus, imposing one additional equation reduces the dimension by one:
$$ \dim(r=2,\Delta=0)=4n-5. $$
$\circ$ The largest lower-rank stratum is $r=1$, which already implies $\Delta=0$,
$\phantom\circ$ and thus still has dimension $3n-1$.
$\circ$ Therefore, by the same argument as before, when
$$ (4n-5)>(3n-1) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\geq5, $$
$\phantom\circ$ it follows that
$R_{n\geq5}/(\langle12\rangle)$ is a domain $\;\Longrightarrow\; \langle12\rangle$ is prime for $n\geq5$
Nagata's Criterion
$\circ$ For $n\geq5$, we have a Noetherian domain $R_n$ containing the prime element $\Delta=\langle12\rangle$.
$\phantom{\circ}$ We now localize at $\Delta$, i.e. we invert it.
$\circ$ Since $\det M=\Delta\neq0$, the momentum-conservation equations
$$ M \begin{pmatrix} c_1\\ c_2 \end{pmatrix} = -\sum_{i=3}^n c_i \begin{pmatrix} a_i\\ b_i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad M \begin{pmatrix} d_1\\ d_2 \end{pmatrix} = -\sum_{i=3}^n d_i \begin{pmatrix} a_i\\ b_i \end{pmatrix} $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ uniquely solve for $c_1,c_2,d_1,d_2$. Therefore
$$ R_n[\Delta^{-1}] \cong P_n[\Delta^{-1}], \qquad P_n= \mathbb F[ a_1,\ldots,a_n, b_1,\ldots,b_n, c_3,\ldots,c_n, d_3,\ldots,d_n ]. $$
$\circ$ The right-hand side is a localization of a polynomial ring, and is therefore a UFD.
$\circ$ Nagata's criterion:
$\phantom\circ$ Let $B$ be a Noetherian domain and $p\in B$ a prime element. If $B[p^{-1}]$ is a UFD, then $B$ is a UFD.
We have: $\quad\Delta$ is prime $\quad+\quad$ $R_n[\Delta^{-1}]$ is UFD $\quad\Longrightarrow\quad$ $R_n$ is UFD.
Theorem: $R_n$ is a unique factorization domain for every $n\geq5$.
Consequences of Factoriality
$\circ$ Unique reduced denominators: for $n\geq5$, any $f\in\operatorname{Frac}(R_n)$ has a reduced representation
$$ f=\frac{N}{D}, \qquad \gcd(N,D)=1, $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ where $D$ is unique up to a nonzero constant, and its factorization $D=\prod_i\mathcal D_i^{q_i}$ is unique up to
$\phantom\circ$ constants and ordering.
$\circ$ For scattering amplitudes: fix a basis of integral / transcendental functions $\{h_i\}$,
$$ \mathcal A^{(\ell)}=\sum_i c_i h_i, \qquad c_i\in\operatorname{Frac}(R_n). $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ If the $h_i$ are linearly independent over $\operatorname{Frac}(R_n)$, each coefficient $c_i$ is uniquely defined and has
$\phantom\circ$ a unique reduced denominator.
Applies to any theory, any loop order, for massless $n\geq5$.

Constructive Factoriality


Factorization Through Principal Localization
$\circ$ More generally, suppose a quotient domain $R=S/J$ admits a principal localization
$$ R[\Delta^{-1}] \cong P[\Delta^{-1}], \qquad P=\mathbb F[y_1,\ldots,y_m]. $$
$\circ$ The localization underlying Nagata's criterion gives a constructive factorization algorithm:
0. take $f\in R$ to be factored;
1. rewrite $f$ in the localized polynomial coordinates as $f=N/\Delta^k$, with $N\in P$;
2. factor $N$ using ordinary polynomial factorization, $N=u\prod_i f_i^{m_i}$;
3. determine the valuations $v_\Delta(f_i)$ using saturation or $p$-adic evaluations in $R$,
$\kern2.3mm$ since the factors $f_i$ may contain hidden powers of $\Delta$;
4. exactly divide out these powers, combine them with $\Delta^{-k}$, and pull the remaining factors back to $R$.
$\circ$ The localization element need not itself be prime:
$\star$ if $\Delta=\prod_\alpha \Delta_\alpha^{e_\alpha}$ with each $\Delta_\alpha$ prime, we simply track the valuations $v_{\Delta_\alpha}$ separately.
$\star$ if a factor $\Delta_\alpha$ is non-prime, the same construction may still produce a valid factorization in $R$,
$\phantom\star$ but irreducibility and uniqueness are no longer guaranteed.
Finding a Localization Automatically
$\circ$ Write $R=S/J=\mathbb F[X]/J$, split the coordinates $X=Y\sqcup Z$,
$\phantom\circ$ and regard the quotient relations over the rational-function field $\mathbb F(Y)$:
$$ J \longrightarrow J^e\subset\mathbb F(Y)[Z]. $$
$\circ$ If the quotient has vector-space dimension one,
$$ \dim_{\mathbb F(Y)} \mathbb F(Y)[Z]/J^e =1, $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ then for every $z\in Z$, there exists $r_z(Y)\in\mathbb F(Y)$ such that
$$ z-r_z(Y)\in J^e, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad z=r_z(Y)\ \bmod J^e . $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ Thus the projection onto the $Y$-coordinates is birational.
$\circ$ Taking a common denominator $D$ of the $r_z(Y)$ gives
$$ R[D^{-1}] \cong \mathbb F[Y,D^{-1}]. $$
$\circ$ For $R_5$, choosing $Z=(c_1,c_2,d_1,d_2)$ gives
$$ D=a_1b_2-a_2b_1=\langle12\rangle . $$
Residue Rings Need Not Be Factorial
$\circ$ For a prime pole $\Delta\in R_n$, residues take values in
$$ \operatorname{Frac}(B_\Delta), \qquad B_\Delta=R_n/(\Delta). $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ Although $R_n$ is a UFD for $n\geq5$, the residue ring $B_\Delta$ need not be factorial.
$\circ$ For example, on $\langle12\rangle=0$, Schouten gives
$$ \langle13\rangle\langle24\rangle = \langle14\rangle\langle23\rangle . $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ The four brackets remain irreducible and pairwise non-associate,
$\phantom{\circ}$ so $B_{\langle12\rangle}$ has genuinely inequivalent factorizations.
$\circ$ This is not exceptional: the residue rings associated with all pole varieties encountered so far in
$\phantom{\circ}$ massless QCD amplitudes through two loops and planar six points are non-factorial, including
$\langle ij\rangle$, $\ \langle i|k+l|j]$, $\ s_{ijkl}$, $\ \Delta_{ij|kl|mn}$.
Least-Degree Denominators in Non-UFD Rings
$\circ$ Suppose the relevant factors belong to a known alphabet $\mathcal A=\{\mathcal D_1,\ldots,\mathcal D_s\}$.
$\phantom\circ$ Encode a multiplicative representation by
$$ e=(e_1,\ldots,e_s)\in\mathbb Z^s, \qquad T_e=\prod_{j=1}^s\mathcal D_j^{e_j}, $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ with positive/negative entries describing numerator/denominator factors.
$\circ$ On a univariate slice $\phi:B_\Delta \to \mathbb{F}_p[t]$, factor the alphabet elements as
$$ \phi(\mathcal D_j) = u_j\prod_{\alpha}p_\alpha(t)^{M_{\alpha j}}, \qquad u_j\in K^\times , $$
$\phantom{\circ}$ where $M_{\alpha j}$ records the multiplicity of the sliced irreducible factor $p_\alpha(t)$.
$\circ$ For a fixed matching $e_0$, all multiplicatively indistinguishable representations on that slice are
$$ e=e_0+\ker_{\mathbb Z}M . $$
$\circ$ Intersect the affine lattices from several slices, then select the admissible representation(s) with
$\phantom\circ$ minimum denominator degree. Non-uniqueness becomes a controlled integer-lattice problem.
